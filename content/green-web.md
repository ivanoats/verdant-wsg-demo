## So What Is the Green Web?

The green web is an internet that is energy-efficient and human-optimized. Energy-efficient means doing the same job with less work. Every page someone loads moves bytes across networks, wakes up processors, and drains a phone battery. A green website sends less, asks the device to do less, and runs on servers powered by renewable energy. That efficiency is found in the architecture of our code, the weight of our pages, and the logic of our development processes.

Human-optimized means it still works for the person on an old phone with a spotty data plan. That&rsquo;s the human case, and it&rsquo;s the one I care about most. Lighter pages load faster, cost less on metered data, and reach people that heavy sites leave out. More often than not, accessibility and sustainability turn out to be the same work. The business case follows from there: faster pages keep more visitors, and smaller sites are cheaper to host and easier to maintain.

## Why I Built Verdant

The W3C Web Sustainability Guidelines tell you what to do. They don&rsquo;t hand you a starting point. On most teams I&rsquo;ve worked with, sustainability shows up late, as an audit after launch, when every fix means undoing a decision someone already made.

I built Verdant to turn that around. It&rsquo;s a small design system and site starter where the defaults are already the sustainable ones: system fonts instead of downloaded ones, only the CSS a page actually uses, dark mode and reduced motion respected out of the box, an offline shell, and byte budgets that fail the build if a page gets heavier. It&rsquo;s also proof. If I&rsquo;m going to tell a client their site can be lighter, I should be able to show them one, measured, with the [source]({{REPO}}) open for anyone to check.

## A Twenty-Year Mission

In 2004, the conversation around &ldquo;green web technology&rdquo; was just beginning. I founded Sustainable Websites with a simple but ambitious goal: to provide a livelihood that was as environmentally responsible as it was technically sound. At the time, that meant pioneering carbon-offset web hosting.

In 2013, after a decade of managing servers, I made a conscious choice to pivot. I sold the hosting business to [GreenGeeks](https://www.greengeeks.com/track/sustainableweb/cp-sw2) to focus on two other passions: raising my children and teaching the next generation of developers.

## The Evolution of Scale

Sustainability is as much about people and processes as it is about power usage. I founded [CodeFellows](https://www.codefellows.org/) to create a more sustainable model for technical education, moving away from the &ldquo;gig economy&rdquo; feel of adjunct professorship to build a lasting ecosystem for knowledge sharing.

For the last six years, I have served as a Senior Software Engineer and Architect at [lululemon](https://shop.lululemon.com/). There, I&rsquo;ve worked at global scale, up to tens of millions of sessions a month at peak, learning how to optimize web development teams, streamline complex processes, and architect systems that serve millions.

## The New Standard: Beyond Offsets

With the recent release of the [W3C Web Sustainability Guidelines](https://www.w3.org/TR/web-sustainability-guidelines/) (WSG), the industry finally has a framework for moving beyond simple carbon offsets to address the root of the problem: digital waste. That framework is arriving at a good moment &mdash; data centers&rsquo; energy and water use is now regularly front-page news, and public patience for it is wearing thin.

The scale of the problem is real. ICT &mdash; the broader category the web sits within &mdash; is estimated to cause 1.8&ndash;2.8% of global greenhouse emissions, according to a Lancaster University study cited by the [Green Web Foundation](https://www.thegreenwebfoundation.org/support/why-does-green-hosting-matter/).
